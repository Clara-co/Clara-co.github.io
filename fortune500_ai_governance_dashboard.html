Key findings for your presentation

🔑 The headline: PR/comm professionals are structurally absent from AI governance
Only 14% of companies include a communication or reputation professional in AI governance — and every single one of those companies is in the Technology sector. Outside of tech, the number is zero across Financial Services, Healthcare, Retail, Media, and Manufacturing. This isn't a resource gap. It's a design choice — and the data suggest it's the wrong one.
⚠️ The governance theater problem: policy without accountability
94% of companies have a public AI policy. But the quality scoring tells a different story. The 0–3 scale (0=absent, 1=aspirational/vague, 2=procedural, 3=measurable/accountable) reveals that most companies cluster around 1–2 on most dimensions — meaning they have language, but not systems. The average score of 12.6 out of 21 reflects governance that looks complete on paper but lacks external audit, published metrics, or board-level accountability. The research team calls this the "governance theater gap."
🏥 Healthcare is the highest-stakes sector with the weakest governance
Healthcare companies (avg score 10.6) are deploying AI in prior authorization, diagnostics, and mental health — and they have the most documented harm. UnitedHealth's nH Predict AI denied post-acute care at 10× the rate of human reviewers. Cigna's PXDX system rejected 300,000+ claims at 1.2 seconds per case. Elevance and Humana face class-action suits over the same pattern. In every case: no comm professional led the response, and governance was strengthened only after the crisis.
📉 Crisis companies show a clear pattern: governance built after the damage
Of the 22% of companies with a documented AI crisis history, the pattern is consistent: vague governance → AI incident → defensive legal response → post-crisis policy update. Amazon scrapped its biased hiring tool. Google published its AI Principles only after the Project Maven employee protest. GM built board-level autonomous vehicle governance after the Cruise pedestrian dragging incident. Netflix formalized GenAI production guidelines only after backlash over undisclosed AI imagery. Reactive governance is not a strategy.
📊 The 10-K vs. website integrity gap
SEC 10-K filings require honest disclosure of AI risk — companies must admit what could go wrong. Corporate websites curate the opposite narrative. Comparing both reveals what the research team calls the "integrity gap": companies that acknowledge material AI risk in legal filings while projecting confidence and ethical leadership in public governance language. Goldman Sachs exemplifies this: strong internal risk controls, but no public-facing responsible AI page. Walmart's 10-K acknowledges technology risk while its website leads with ethical AI commitments.
✅ What good looks like: the 5-company benchmark
Salesforce (20/21), Microsoft, Google, IBM, and Cisco (all 19/21) define the ceiling. Common factors: comm/policy professionals with voting seats on AI governance panels; published third-party audits or external research partnerships; board-level reporting; AI ethics infrastructure that predates most peers by 3–5 years; crisis response protocols documented in advance. These companies treated AI governance as a reputational infrastructure problem — not just a technical one.

Implications for communication & reputation professionals

The case you need to make internally
The companies where comm professionals have a voting governance seat score an average of 19/21. The companies where they have no role score an average of 10.5/21. This is not correlation — it reflects that stakeholder trust infrastructure (Q7), transparency (Q1), and crisis preparedness (Q6) require communication expertise to build. Technical teams alone don't design for public perception.
What audiences will ask about
Conference audiences (PRSA ICON, PAGE Society, IABC) will want to know: How do I get a seat at the table? What language do I use with the C-suite? What does a voting governance role actually look like? The Salesforce quote is your anchor: governance principles were approved by executives "including co-CEOs and marketing" — that's how you name the role without sounding like an advocate for yourself.
Methodological credibility for Q&A
The IRR log shows Cohen's Kappa = 1.00 on all binary variables (policy exists, governance body named, comm role) across 15 randomly selected companies. Quality score agreement is 97%+ within 1 point. That's above the 0.70 publishability threshold. When peer reviewers or conference attendees ask how you know the coding is reliable, this is your answer.
